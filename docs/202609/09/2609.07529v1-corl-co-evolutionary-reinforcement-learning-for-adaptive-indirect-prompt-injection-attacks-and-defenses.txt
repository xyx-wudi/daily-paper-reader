## ABSTRACT 

Tool-augmented language agents are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection (IPI). Unlike direct prompt injection, IPI hides adversarial instructions in untrusted tool outputs and can covertly alter the execution of a legitimate task. Defenses trained on fixed attacks may fail as an attacker changes its strategy, injection site, and payload. To address this problem, we formulate adaptive IPI as an asymmetric, partially observable, general-sum Markov game: a multi-turn attacker adapts pay-loads at reached tool-return sites from the public trajectory, while a tool-using de-fender must block the injected objective and complete the user task. We propose CoRL, a verifier-grounded co-evolution and repair framework with three stages: Attacker SFT initializes multi-turn attacks from successful trajectories; bilateral Co-PPO jointly trains both agents with role-specific rewards and historical oppo-nent populations; and Defender SFT consolidates verifier-accepted teacher repairs for population-discovered failures. Across 1,514 clean, fixed-template, and adap-tive executions per defender, CoRL reduces overall ASR by 38.5 points to 0.0% and raises utility by 13.1 points to 76.3%. Stage-wise and controlled ablations show positive contributions from online Co-PPO and population-mined repair, while external-benchmark evaluation indicates transfer in attack resistance. The defender balances safety and task utility under the evaluated attacks, while the re-tained attackers provide candidates for adaptive red-team evaluation. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Tool-augmented language agents consume untrusted webpages, emails, documents, and application state while calling tools that can modify external environments. Their tool returns can therefore mix task data with third-party instructions. Indirect prompt injection (IPI) exploits this ambiguity to redirect legitimate execution toward data leakage, tool misuse, or unauthorized actions (Greshake et al., 2023; Zhan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024). A useful defense must block the injected objective while completing the user’s task: rejecting external content wholesale may appear safe, but destroys the agent’s utility. Despite increasingly stateful IPI benchmarks (Zhan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026), defenses are often trained on fixed or weakly varying attacks. Such defenses may not generalize when attackers condition payloads on the public trajectory, the reached injection site, and prior attempts. Online co-training introduces a second difficulty: each current policy can overfit to its latest opponent and forget earlier attack or defense modes. Meanwhile, optimizing attack blocking without an explicit task-utility signal can favor blanket refusal (Zhan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; He et al., 2026). These limitations leave a common challenge: how can training continually discover adaptive failures and retain their repairs in a deployable defender? We address this challenge through a discovery-and-repair view: discovery exposes failures as both policies change, while repair retains their fixes without inducing blanket refusal. We model the within-execution interaction as an asymmetric, partially observable, general-sum Markov game. Independent verifiers score attack success and task completion, defining payoffs that reward the attacker for compromise but distinguish safe task completion, safe failure, and compromise for the defender. Because these payoffs are not exact negatives, the game is general-sum. We instantiate it in an AgentDyn-derived seven-domain environment, holding out official configurations for evaluation. We propose CoRL , a three-stage realization of this view. Attacker SFT first bootstraps multi-turn exploration from verifier-validated successes. Online Co-PPO then updates both roles with role-specific PPO against bilateral historical populations, with the aim of reducing latest-opponent over-fitting while improving the defender and retaining attackers that expose residual failures. Finally, 

Defender SFT starts from the selected Co-PPO checkpoint and learns verified safe task execution from teachers facing this population. Across seven domains and 1,514 raw executions per defender, Co-PPO improves every reported met-ric over its no-population counterpart, although the larger final gain follows Defender SFT. Relative to the base model, complete CoRL reduces observed overall ASR from 38.5% to 0.0% and raises overall utility from 63.2% to 76.3% under our protocols. A controlled 2 ×2 study shows positive contributions from Co-PPO initialization and population-mined repairs, with their combination per-forming best. External evaluations on InjecAgent and AgentLAB support transfer beyond the main benchmark, including improved task success under the mixed-attacker Task-Injection protocol on AgentLAB. Our contributions are: 

(1) We formulate within-execution adaptive IPI as an asymmetric general-sum Markov game and build a seven-domain, verifier-grounded environment; we will open-source the environment and SFT/RL data under responsible-release safeguards. 

(2) We introduce CoRL, to our knowledge the first adaptive-IPI framework to train both an attacker agent and a tool-using defender agent with RL while exposing each current policy to historical op-ponents; Defender SFT then consolidates population-mined repairs. 

(3) Across seven suites, InjecAgent, and AgentLAB, we report safety–utility, controlled attribution, stronger-attack tests, and stage-wise dynamics; CoRL leads the tested same-backbone baselines. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

IPI benchmarks and defenses. Benchmarks including InjecAgent, AgentDojo, Agent Security Bench, AgentDyn, and AgentLAB evaluate IPI in increasingly stateful or long-horizon tool-use en-vironments (Zhan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026). Defenses span provenance marking (Hines et al., 2024), structured input or alignment (Chen et al., 2025a; Wallace et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b;c), prompt guards (Li et al., 2025b), and system-level control-flow constraints (Debenedetti et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Many are evaluated primarily against fixed attack distributions; adaptive evaluations show that some defenses can degrade when attacks are optimized against the deployed system (Zhan et al., 2025). This motivates training and evaluation that jointly measure security and legitimate task completion. 

Adaptive attack discovery and repair. Automated red-teaming broadens the failures available for safety training. Lee et al. (2025) train a GFlowNet attacker with MLE smoothing to generate diverse harmful prompts, then safety-tune a fixed target on the discovered attacks. Dabas et al. (2026) instead compose primitives from a learned jailbreak dictionary to improve robustness to unseen attacks. In IPI, AutoInject and PISmith optimize transferable injections with RL (Chen et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026), while RETA generates diversity-aware attacks against a frozen baseline before multi-objective defender RL (He et al., 2026). These methods establish the value of attack coverage and downstream repair, but do not jointly evolve an attacker and a tool-using defender within the same adaptive IPI execution. 

Attacker–defender co-evolution. Wang et al. (2025a) alternate attacker refinement with refusal-trained Defender updates using single-turn attacks; MAGIC studies multi-turn co-evolution for gen-eral LLM safety (Wen et al., 2026); and GPT-Red scales prompt-injection self-play against diverse simultaneously trained defenders (Wallace et al., 2026). ARLAS is the closest IPI-specific prior work: it co-trains an attacker and tool agent but gives historical opponents only to the defender (Wang et al., 2025b). CoRL’s distinction is therefore not co-evolution alone. To our knowledge, it is the first adaptive-IPI framework to optimize both current roles against historical opponents in-side a shared tool-use execution, then reuse retained attackers to collect verifier-approved defender demonstrations. Appendix A gives the detailed comparison.

## 4 METHOD 

CoRL is the complete discovery-and-repair framework, whereas Co-PPO is its online co-evolution stage. Attacker SFT supplies a starting policy for exploration; Co-PPO improves both agents against current and historical opponents; Defender SFT learns verified successful teacher trajectories under the retained attacks. The online stage thus supplies both the defender initialization and the attacks used to collect demonstrations; Q3 tests these two inputs under a matched SFT recipe. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We ask five questions: Q1 , does CoRL improve safety and task utility; Q2 , do gains extend across suites and external benchmarks; Q3 , what do online initialization and population-derived repair data contribute; Q4 , do gains persist across historical attackers; and Q5 , does Co-PPO strengthen the attacker and support competitive adaptation? 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Implementation. SFT uses 16 GPUs per stage with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3; Co-PPO uses 56 GPUs with verl/Megatron, vLLM, and Ray. Appendix G details the allocation. 

Evaluation workloads. The main test manifest combines three AgentDyn and four AgentDojo suites (Li et al., 2026; Debenedetti et al., 2024): 157 user tasks and 55 injection tasks yield 1,189 within-suite task–goal cases. Excluding two pre-declared inconsistent cases leaves 1,187 metric-eligible adaptive executions. We also run 157 clean tasks and 168 fixed-template executions: six cases per suite under each of four official templates. Each fully covered defender therefore has 1,514 raw executions and 1,512 eligible records (157 clean, 168 fixed, 1,187 adaptive). Appendix D spec-ifies the templates and suite counts; the broader single-template evaluation in Q2 is reported sepa-rately. The common-adaptive condition freezes one selected Co-PPO attacker across defenders; within each independent execution, it still adapts payloads to the public trajectory at reached sites. Q4 evalu-ates the per-case success union over historical attackers, whose population also supplies attacks for Defender-SFT data collection.

## 6 LIMITATIONS 

Our study has three main limitations. First, training and evaluation cover a finite budget of attack goals; our sandbox uses fixed, deterministic security and task verifiers. Unenumerated goals or veri-fier misspecification may therefore escape both training and evaluation. Second, historical-opponent populations broaden policy exposure, but Co-PPO neither computes a meta-strategy nor estimates ex-ploitability and thus offers no guarantee of convergence to an equilibrium. Third, both roles depend on teacher supervision: Attacker SFT uses successful attacks, while Defender SFT uses safe task-execution demonstrations. These results do not establish whether co-evolution alone, self-generated supervision, or weaker teachers can achieve comparable performance. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

CoRL combines adaptive failure discovery through bilateral online training with population-guided supervised repair. Controlled SFT comparisons indicate that both the online defender initialization and population-derived demonstrations contribute to the final gain, with their combination perform-ing best. It achieves 75.40% adaptive Safe-U and zero observed common-attacker ASR across seven suites, with safety and task-success gains on AgentLAB. The historical attacker pool and AgentLAB nevertheless yield nonzero ASR, exposing the limits of the common-attacker result. ## A ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK 

IPI benchmarks and dynamic evaluation. Indirect prompt injection arises when untrusted exter-nal content is interpreted as actionable instructions by an LLM agent. Early work showed that LLM-integrated applications blur the boundary between data and instructions, enabling attacks through webpages, emails, retrieved documents, and other third-party content (Greshake et al., 2023). Later benchmarks instantiate this threat in increasingly realistic settings. InjecAgent studies IPI in tool-integrated agents (Zhan et al., 2024); AgentDojo provides stateful task environments for evaluating attacks and defenses (Debenedetti et al., 2024); and Agent Security Bench covers broader agent-security risks (Zhang et al., 2025). AgentDyn moves closer to deployment realism by emphasizing open-ended user tasks, helpful third-party instructions, and longer multi-application workflows (Li et al., 2026), while AgentLAB targets adaptive long-horizon attacks, including an AgentDojo-based Task Injection track (Jiang et al., 2026). Our work follows this direction but focuses on training-time coupling: defender policies learn while tasks, observations, attack placements, victim configurations, and payload strategies vary within the same verifier-controlled training loop. 

IPI defenses and the safety–utility tradeoff. Most IPI defenses aim to restore the instruction– data boundary. Prompt-level methods such as Spotlighting use provenance signals to mark external content (Hines et al., 2024). Structured-input and model-level methods, including StruQ, Instruction Hierarchy, SecAlign, and Meta SecAlign, separate instruction and data channels or train models to follow higher-priority instructions (Chen et al., 2025a; Wallace et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b;c). System-level approaches such as CaMeL impose control-flow, data-flow, and capability constraints around the model (Debenedetti et al., 2025). RETA trains a defender to reason about whether em-bedded instructions align with the user task and uses red-team generation plus multi-objective RL to improve the safety–utility trade-off (He et al., 2026). These approaches reduce the influence of malicious instructions, but IPI defense is not only a refusal or filtering problem. In realistic agent workflows, third-party content may contain both information necessary for task completion and ad-versarial instructions that should be ignored. A secure agent must preserve useful content while rejecting malicious control, motivating joint security–utility verifier rewards for defender training. 

Diverse red-teaming and repair for unseen attacks. Attack diversity is a complementary route to robust safety training. Lee et al. (2025) show that conventional RL red-teamers can collapse onto a few modes; their GFlowNet-plus-MLE procedure discovers diverse attacks against a fixed target, after which refusal SFT on those prompts improves robustness to attacks from other red-teamers. Dabas et al. (2026) take a data-centric approach: they extract recurring skills from prior jailbreaks, learn a compact dictionary, and train on novel skill compositions to improve generalization to unseen attacks while monitoring over-refusal. Neither approach jointly updates an attacker and defender within an agent execution. CoRL shares their discovery-to-repair motivation: its evolving attackers expose tool-use vulnerabilities and then challenge teacher defenders, whose safe, task-successful trajectories provide supervision for the deployed defender. 

Adaptive attacks and attacker–defender co-evolution. Static attack sets are insufficient because attackers adapt to deployed systems (Zhan et al., 2025; Nasr et al., 2025); automated jailbreak meth-ods such as GCG, PAIR, and TAP reinforce this concern (Zou et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2023; Mehro-tra et al., 2024). AutoInject learns universal transferable injections with black-box RL, and PISmith trains a black-box RL red-teamer against prompt-injection defenses, including agentic settings (Chen et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026). These methods provide important independently optimized attacker baselines, but do not jointly evolve an IPI defender in the same multi-injection tool-use trajectory. RETA generates diversity-aware red-team data against a frozen baseline before optimizing the de-fender (He et al., 2026). At the level of the training loop, Wang et al. (2025a) provide the closest accepted precedent: Lifelong Safety Alignment mines strategies from jailbreak papers, refines a Meta-Attacker against a frozen De-fender, and then refusal-trains the Defender on accumulated successful attacks over iterative rounds. This establishes attacker–defender evolution as a prior safety-alignment paradigm. Its experiments use single-turn jailbreak prompts and update one role against a frozen counterpart within each sub-stage, rather than modeling adaptive interventions inside a tool-use trajectory or training against his-torical opponent populations. MAGIC likewise studies attacker–defender learning for general LLM safety rather than tool-return IPI (Wen et al., 2026). GPT-Red scales prompt-injection self-play 13 Preprint against simultaneously trained defenders, but refines candidates through a defender-query harness and inserts selected attacks at predetermined locations (Wallace et al., 2026); it does not train both current roles against historical opponents across multiple reached sites in one execution. ARLAS is the closest IPI-specific prior work: it co-trains an attacker and tool agent, generates an injection at each environment step, and trains the latest defender against earlier attackers while the current attacker faces the latest defender (Wang et al., 2025b). CoRL instead acts only at reached tool-return sites and gives later attacker turns the benign return, public execution trace, earlier payloads, and intervening defender behavior. It further exposes both current roles to historical opponents, uses independent executable security and utility verifiers, and reuses retained attackers for teacher demonstration collection. Our NoPop comparison removes historical exposure from both roles; it does not separately isolate the benefit over a one-sided population. 

Population-based adversarial learning. Training against mixtures of current and historical oppo-nents can reduce specialization to a single latest policy, as explored by PSRO and prioritized fictitious self-play (Lanctot et al., 2017; Vinyals et al., 2019). CoRL adopts historical-opponent exposure for a general-sum IPI game, but does not compute a meta-strategy or claim convergence to an equi-librium. Establishing absolute checkpoint strength or reduced forgetting would additionally require frozen cross-play on common tasks and seeds. 

Multi-turn agent RL and reward design. AgentGym-RL studies reinforcement learning for long-horizon, multi-turn agents across interactive environments (Xi et al., 2026), while tool-call reward models introduce turn-level shaping and credit assignment for tool invocation (Ma et al., 2026). Rule-Based Rewards use composable, LLM-graded rules to align helpfulness and safety (Mu et al., 2024). CoRL instead uses deterministic task-specific security and utility verifiers as terminal rewards and propagates them only along the corresponding role-generated token timeline.

## C INTERLEAVED ATTACKER PROMPT AND EXECUTION DETAILS 

Attacker prompt and response format. The attacker system instruction frames payload genera-tion as controlled red-team evaluation and requires a serialized assistant response containing private rationale in <think> tags followed by the candidate payload in <attack> tags. To bootstrap reliable exploration, it lists a small set of previously effective patterns: structured tool-call blocks, workflow prerequisites, administrative notices, JSON-like action fields, colleague requests, verifica-tion framing, and failure-recovery framing. These are prompt suggestions rather than a rollout-time routing variable: the online policy receives no attack-family label and chooses a concrete strategy from the objective, authoritative executable contract, public trace, and local tool-return context. The instruction suggests approximately 300–800 payload characters but does not impose a character-level hard cap. Generation is bounded by 3,072 attacker response tokens, and sanitization removes unsupported or control characters without truncating otherwise valid payload text. Tool-response truncation is disabled in the current launcher; a request that cannot satisfy the configured context budget fails closed instead of silently changing its target or history. At exact-field-withheld evaluation, the environment still parses the authoritative contract for deter-ministic verification and telemetry, but removes the TARGET TOOL CALL/OUTPUT fields and the repeated exact-call specification from the attacker-visible generation instruction. The natural-language objective, public trace, suite/domain hint, local tool-return context, previous-attempt his-tory, and binary unsatisfied-contract feedback are unchanged. The evaluated policy is the selected Co-PPO attacker checkpoint, frozen for the common defender comparison after exposure to exe-cutable contracts during online RL; no objective-only checkpoint is trained. 

Observable context. At the k-th triggered injection site, the user message contains a normalized natural-language attack objective, its authoritative sequence of zero or more target calls with ex-act function names and arguments, a suite hint, the current tool name, benign text surrounding the placeholder, and the public defender execution trace. The user task appears through the trace rather than as a duplicate field. Serialization has a 6,000-character context budget; when a trace exceeds this budget, it retains a compact prefix containing task context and a larger recent suffix contain-ing behavior relevant to the current injection. The trace includes public assistant outputs, parsed tool names and arguments, and tool-response excerpts. Explicit <think> blocks and API-side 

reasoning_content fields are removed before serialization; the defender system prompt, in-ternal activations, model parameters, checkpoint identity, global environment state, complete tool schemas, and untriggered injection sites are unavailable. 

Previous-attempt context. For k > 1, the attacker conversation retains earlier user messages and complete serialized attacker responses, including private attacker rationale. The next prompt sum-marizes the defender’s observable actions following the preceding payload and provides a binary indication that the authoritative target contract remains unsatisfied at the next trigger. This is limited 16 Preprint intermediate verifier feedback: the attacker receives no graded reward, successful-call decompo-sition, terminal-state details, or hidden defender reasoning before termination. This attacker-side history is never exposed to the defender. 

Strict placeholder replacement. Each configured injection site receives a unique inert placeholder identifier and is recorded in a handled-site set. When a returned field contains one or more unhandled placeholders, the rollout obtains a response for each triggered site, replaces only the corresponding placeholder, and preserves the surrounding benign content. A site cannot trigger a second attacker ac-tion even if the same tool is called again. An empty response, explicit refusal, incomplete <attack> 

block, or other format violation is attributed to the model; the placeholder is removed without insert-ing alternative instructions. If the exact sampled-token trace remains valid, that response stays in attacker PPO and receives the shared terminal trajectory reward; absent a later successful attempt, its malformed output makes the format bonus zero. Trace-alignment and evaluator failures instead invalidate the affected sample and are reported separately. 

Attacker initialization. On Co-PPO’s training-split configurations, DeepSeek V4 Pro and Seed2.0 act as teacher attackers against the base defender using the attacker system instruction, ChatML schema, public-trace serialization, and multi-turn history format. The security verifier retains 3,995 conversations satisfying the executable attack contract (11,655 attacker turns). Domain-OOD. The default validation set is not domain-OOD. We use the term only for separately trained leave-one-domain-out models whose held-out domain contributes no SFT or online-RL data. The seven-domain model is therefore not described as domain-OOD on any of these domains. 

Injection-OOD. The default validation set is also not injection-OOD. We apply this label only when the corresponding attack-goal family, compromised field or site family, and construction tem-plate are excluded from Attacker SFT, Co-PPO, and Defender SFT. Merely holding out an exact configuration does not establish injection-OOD transfer. 

Payload-OOD. InjecAgent supplies independently authored fixed payloads and therefore provides evidence for payload-source transfer. We do not call it strict payload-OOD unless a construction audit confirms that its goal families, compromised fields, sites, and templates are absent from all SFT and online-RL data. 

External-benchmark transfer. Tables 13 and 15 report complete AgentLAB coverage and the available InjecAgent coverage. AgentLAB provides separate attack-success and attacked-task-success outcomes, whereas the InjecAgent results measure attack resistance. G.5 CO-PPO SAFE REPLICA RELOAD AND RECOVERY During Co-PPO, historical-model reload is treated as a serving-state transition. The rollouter first stops assigning new requests to the affected historical role, routes that traffic through current–current pairs, and waits for all active requests to drain before restarting replicas. Checkpoint attribution is fixed when a request is issued, even if the serving URL is later reloaded. If a restart fails, only that historical role remains disabled until reload succeeds; current-policy training continues. Co-PPO population state is checkpointed together with training state. It includes candidate check-points, rolling and per-suite reward histories, fitness and sample counts, loaded slot mappings, last up-date steps, and random-number-generator state. On resume, the saved historical models are reloaded into their previous slots rather than resetting every slot to the base model. ## H VERIFIER RELIABILITY AND IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Rule-based verification. The sandbox uses deterministic verifiers rather than LLM judges for PPO rewards and main-benchmark evaluation. Depending on the task, success is checked through function-call traces or post-environment state predicates; the additional teacher-demonstration qual-ity screen is documented separately and does not replace these outcome verifiers. Determinism re-moves judge variance but does not guarantee specification correctness. An independent human audit of both outcome verifiers is not yet available; the reported sandbox metrics therefore measure com-pliance with these executable specifications. Evaluator exceptions and exact-trace failures remain separate invalid samples rather than negative rewards. 

Training implementation. The attacker base model is first trained by trajectory-success-filtered all-turn SFT on verifier-approved interleaved conversations generated by DeepSeek V4 Pro and Seed2.0. Bilateral Co-PPO then uses one online rollout per sampled row and separate value critics for both online roles. After Co-PPO checkpoint selection, retained attackers challenge teacher defenders running complete tasks; verifier-approved demonstrations then train the final defender with masked cross-entropy. No online optimizer is resumed afterward. During Co-PPO, the implementation scans each full serialized PPO trajectory with a role-specific response mask: at a masked position it car-ries the next valid value and GAE accumulator backward unchanged, without advancing that role’s effective timeline. Both CoRL actors use the low-variance KL estimator and sequence-mean/token-sum normalization. Co-PPO rollouts are generated asynchronously. Workers execute each defender task once and pause only at triggered injection sites; trajectories feed role-specific attacker and defender trainer queues. Producer-role masks ensure that a trainer optimizes only its own generated tokens. Each request records immutable producer-role and checkpoint-version metadata; the active trainer rejects mal-formed metadata and samples whose producer policy is more than one version stale. Parsed payloads re-entering later context, tool outputs, opponent actions, and other environment tokens are masked from actor losses. The defender uses γ = 1 .0, λ = 0 .95 , asymmetric clipping (0 .20 , 0.24) , KL coef-ficient 0.001 , and 100 critic-only trainer updates before actor training. The attacker uses asymmetric clipping (0 .20 , 0.28) , KL coefficient 0.001 , and 40 critic-only trainer updates; dataset composition is reported in Section 4.2 and Table 16. During Co-PPO, the configured historical capacity and physical serving allocation are four check-points per role. One slot is reserved for a provisional candidate until it obtains at least 32 role-attributable, reward-bearing evaluations in each of seven suites. Each suite retains up to 200 recent rewards, and candidate fitness is their suite-macro mean. Eligible checkpoints are sampled without replacement with shifted fitness weights; top-half filtering begins only when the eligible pool con-tains at least twice the number of elite slots. Checkpoints are saved every 10 policy versions and populations are considered for update every 20 versions. Candidate histories, slot mappings, update steps, and random-number-generator state are restored with the Co-PPO training checkpoint. 31
