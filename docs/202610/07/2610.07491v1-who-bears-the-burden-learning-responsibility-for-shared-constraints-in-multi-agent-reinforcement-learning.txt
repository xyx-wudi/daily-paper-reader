# ABSTRACT 

When multiple agents share a cost budget, a common Lagrange multiplier can en-force the aggregate constraint but does not determine how its penalty should be allocated across agents. Uniform penalties ignore heterogeneity in the rewards agents sacrifice, while agent-specific multipliers may still rely on the same ag-gregate cost signal. We introduce Lagrang ian Responsibility Allocation (LiRA), which learns each agent’s share of a common multiplier by optimizing social wel-fare over a finite training horizon. The multiplier enforces the aggregate budget, while responsibility shares redistribute its influence without modifying the orig-inal rewards or constraints. For convex games under standard regularity condi-tions, varying these shares induces a smooth family of normalized generalized Nash equilibria in which active constraints remain at their budgets while wel-fare varies. To optimize responsibility before convergence, we derive a welfare gradient that accounts for both learning updates and the induced change in data distribution. Across CityLearn, MABIM, Harvest, and MetaDrive, spanning 3 to 400 agents, LiRA improves average social welfare by up to 29% over uniform and agent-specific multiplier baselines. Grid and driving costs remain within budget, inventory violations decrease, and Harvest makes more effective use of available budget. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Consider two delivery robots approaching a narrow corridor. One carries an urgent package, while the other is traveling to collect a package. Either robot may yield to avoid a collision, but delaying the loaded robot incurs a larger loss. A shared collision-avoidance constraint excludes unsafe outcomes, but does not determine how the burden of satisfying the constraint should be allocated between the robots. This ambiguity arises more broadly in multi-agent systems subject to shared constraints, including power limits, resource budgets, and safety requirements, when agents incur heterogeneous costs for adapting their behavior. A shared Lagrange multiplier specifies the strength of the penalty associated with a common con-straint, but does not determine how that penalty should be apportioned across agents. Applying the same penalty to all agents leaves this allocation implicit. Introducing agent-specific multipliers does not resolve the issue when each multiplier is driven by the same aggregate constraint cost. We instead make the allocation of constraint responsibility explicit. Specifically, a responsibility share determines the fraction of the shared penalty applied to each agent’s objective, while the common multiplier continues to enforce the aggregate constraint. The original rewards and constraint budget remain unchanged. Responsibility allocation therefore modifies which agents are incentivized to adapt without altering the underlying constrained problem.

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Shared safety in multi-agent learning. Constrained reinforcement learning trades reward against explicit cost budgets (Altman, 1999). Single-agent methods enforce constraints via trust regions (Achiam et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2020b) or Lagrangian penalties (Tessler et al., 2019; Stooke et al., 2020). With multiple agents, one learner’s update shifts the feasible set for the others. Constrained policy optimization and decentralized descent–ascent address these coupled dynamics (Gu et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2021), and regret and violation bounds exist for certain two-player settings (Ding et al., 2023). All of these methods enforce the shared cost, but none asks which agents should absorb it. In multi-agent control, responsibility allocations inferred from demonstrations through control barrier functions explain how much each agent deviates to keep an interaction safe (Remy et al., 2025). LiRA keeps the shared cost feedback and instead learns that distributional choice for social welfare during reinforcement learning. 

Equilibria under shared constraints. When agents share constraints, the feasible set of each depends on the others’ choices. Generalized Nash equilibria formalize such interdependence (Facchinei & Kanzow, 2010). Rosen’s normalized equilibria fix the ratios between agents’ con-straint multipliers (Rosen, 1965) and parameterized variational inequalities trace the resulting family (Nabetani et al., 2011; Dreves, 2018). In dynamic games with shared constraints, non-normalized equilibria can also be computed and a preferred one selected by a design criterion (Pustilnik & Borrelli, 2025). Recent safe-learning work studies convergence to such equilibria under state-wise constraints (Li & Azizan, 2024). Our responsibility shares set the multiplier ratios directly. Welfare then selects among the equilibria they index. 

Learning through the agents’ response. Varying allocations can improve welfare at equilibrium, but agents are still learning when shares must be chosen. Incentive design modifies rewards in anticipation of agents’ adaptive response (Yang et al., 2020a; 2022; Zheng et al., 2022). Bilevel formulations and differentiable equilibrium blocks offer complementary approaches (Thoma et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Thoma et al., 2026). LiRA differs in that it allocates the penalty of an existing constraint rather than designing new rewards. Its gradient combines meta-gradient differ-entiation through learner updates (Xu et al., 2018; Franceschi et al., 2017; Grefenstette et al., 2019) with a sampling correction for the data collected by intermediate policies (Vuorio et al., 2022). Iter-ative and implicit hypergradients connect finite-window responses to equilibrium sensitivity (Grazzi et al., 2020).

In Figure 1(a), either robot can yield. A feasible outcome need not be the best one for their com-bined rewards. The constraint says what is forbidden, not who should give way. Generalized Nash equilibria formalize such constrained best responses (Facchinei & Kanzow, 2010). Among them, a normalized equilibrium fixes the ratios of agents’ constraint multipliers (Rosen, 1965). The respon-sibility shares set exactly those ratios, so changing ρ selects a different equilibrium under the same shared constraint. When their marginal rewards for yielding differ, the joint policy shifts along the active cost surface. Total cost stays at the budget because the shared multiplier compensates, but the agents divide the adjustment differently (Figure 1(b)). The theorem makes this precise through the nonzero welfare derivative. It does not guarantee that every direction improves welfare, only that some direction does whenever the derivative is nonzero. # 4 EXPERIMENTS 

The theory above assumes convex games with smooth payoffs. We now test whether LiRA produces welfare gains when agents train with nonlinear function approximators for a finite time. Four tasks test welfare and cost across different scales and constraint types. CityLearn then lets us inspect individual agents. Throughout, we read costs alongside rewards to distinguish gains obtained by spending unused budget from gains at nearly the same shared cost. 4.1 WELFARE AND SHARED COSTS ACROSS TASKS 

The shared constraint plays a different role in each of our four tasks. In CityLearn v2 (Nweye et al., 2025), three buildings control their energy devices for thermal comfort. They draw electricity from 6Preprint the same district, so their combined net demand faces one grid cap: reducing demand in one building can relieve a cost borne by all three. In MABIM (Yang et al., 2023), 400 product agents manage replenishment across an inventory network to maximize total profit, subject to shared order-rejection caps across two warehouse-store links. An allocation now distributes pressure across hundreds of decisions and two distinct constraints. Harvest, the Melting Pot (Agapiou et al., 2022) substrate commons harvest open (built on Lab2D (Beattie et al., 2020)), has seven agents collecting a common resource under one shared cost. MetaDrive (Li et al., 2023) has four driving agents and a shared limit on the rate of safety events. These tasks differ in scale and in what an agent gives up to reduce cost. In all four benchmarks, we set αi = 1 for every agent, so welfare is the sum of agents’ original returns. We compare LiRA with two baselines. Uniform responsibility sets ρik = 1 /N , and each agent receives an equal share of each shared multiplier. The per-agent Lagrangian baseline (PAL) instead learns a separate multiplier for each agent. LiRA keeps a shared multiplier and learns its respon-sibility shares. Within each task, methods use the same inner learner and matched training seeds under a common evaluation protocol. Each CityLearn building earns a thermal-comfort reward. The shared cost accumulates the positive excess of district net electricity over a per-step grid cap across 719 steps, with a budget of 28 .76 kWh. For an overcooled building, easing cooling serves both objectives at once. It saves electricity and improves comfort. For a building that is already too warm, the comfort effect reverses. Under Uniform, Building 1 is below the comfort band for 314 steps and Building 2 for 232 . Build-ing 3 is above it for 318 . The first two buildings are overcooled, while the third tends to run warm. LiRA moves Building 1’s daytime temperatures toward the comfort band. # 5 CONCLUSION 

A shared cost limit constrains what agents may do collectively but leaves open who adjusts. LiRA makes this division learnable. At equilibrium, responsibility shares can change welfare even as active costs stay in their budgets. The finite-training gradient extends this to nonconvergent learners by estimating welfare’s response to a proposed allocation through independent lookaheads. Across four tasks, the learned shares give the highest mean welfare among the compared methods. In CityLearn, the welfare gain traces to individual buildings: the two that overcool receive a larger penalty share, cool less, and recover comfort while district electricity falls. 

# 6 LIMITATIONS 

Theorem 1 requires own-concave payoffs, convex shared costs, and a strongly monotone weighted payoff field with a fixed active-constraint set. Deep policy networks violate these conditions, and our finite-training gradient (Theorem 2) does not guarantee welfare improvement in such settings by itself. Closing this gap, for instance, by establishing local improvement guarantees for nonconvex learners under shared constraints, is a substantial open problem. The responsibility shares are also constant across states; every state sees the same allocation. A state-dependent ρ(s) could, for exam-ple, shift responsibility during peak-demand hours, but it turns the outer problem from optimizing a 9Preprint point on the simplex into learning a function, and the welfare gradient would need to track how the allocation itself reshapes the state distribution.

Before the outer optimizer, this scales Eq. (10)’s unnormalized score estimate by 1/12 .
