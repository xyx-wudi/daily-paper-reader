# Fully Online Decentralized Learning in Stochastic Games with Unknown Independent Chains 

S. Rasoul Etesami ETESAMI 1@ ILLINOIS .EDU 

Department of Industrial and Systems Engineering, Coordinated Science Laboratory University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, IL, USA 61801 

Abstract 

We consider stochastic games with independent controlled chains and unknown transition kernels, in which players observe only their own local states and realized payoffs. We develop a fully online decentralized and uncoordinated mirror-descent algorithm that operates in the dual space of occupancy measures for approximating stationary Nash equilibrium (NE) policies. The algorithm uses a single transition/reward sample at every primitive time step, allows players to update using only local information, and requires neither coverage of the joint state space nor synchronized episodes. Under standard uniform-ergodicity and finite-coverage assumptions, we show that, with high probability, the time-averaged fixed-comparator regret decays at the canonical eO(T −1/2) rate, up to logarithmic factors and polynomial dependence on the game parameters. In particular, the complexity depends on the cover times of the individual local state spaces rather than on that of the product state space, thereby avoiding exponential dependence on the number of players and the sizes of the joint state and action spaces. We further show that the resulting finite-time regret bound provides an approximate coarse-correlated-equilibrium guarantee. Such a guarantee is natural for arbitrary reward functions, since, in this setting, computing a stationary ϵ-Nash equilibrium ( ϵ-NE) is known to be PPAD-hard. Under an additional global variational-stability condition, we show that the same fully online algorithm converges asymptotically in the last iterate to a stationary ϵ-NE. Our results thus provide the first fully online and scalable learning framework for stochastic games with unknown independent chains. Our algorithmic development can also be viewed as a primal-dual framework for Markov games that fully exploits the independence and local structure of the players’ controlled transition chains and may be of independent interest. 

Keywords: Stochastic games; independent chains; stationary Nash equilibrium; online decentral-ized learning; occupancy measures; unknown transition kernels; coarse-correlated equilibrium. 

1 Introduction 

Learning in multi-agent systems becomes particularly challenging when strategic interactions, dy-namic stochastic state evolution, and limited information are present simultaneously (Zhang et al., 2021). Unlike in a single-agent Markov decision process (Altman, 2021), where the environment is typically viewed as exogenous, each agent in a stochastic game faces an environment that is partly shaped by the evolving decisions of other strategic agents (Shapley, 1953; Bas ¸ar and Olsder, 1999). Consequently, even when the underlying transition dynamics are stationary, the effective op-timization problem faced by each player changes as the other players adapt. This coupling raises a fundamental question in decentralized learning: under what structural conditions can self-interested agents, using only locally available observations, learn meaningful equilibrium behavior without coordination or knowledge of the underlying model? For general-sum games (Nash, 1950), one cannot expect an affirmative answer without addi-tional structure. Computing a Nash equilibrium (NE) is PPAD-hard even for finite normal-form games (Daskalakis et al., 2009), and stochastic games contain such static games as special cases. 

1arXiv:2610.01181v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Much of the algorithmic literature has therefore focused on structured settings in which equilib-rium learning becomes tractable (Mertikopoulos and Zhou, 2019). Examples include two-player zero-sum stochastic games, where fictitious-play or independent-learning procedures can achieve asymptotic or finite-sample convergence guarantees (Daskalakis et al., 2020; Sayin et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022), and Markov potential games (MPGs), where the potential structure can be ex-ploited to establish global convergence guarantees for policy-gradient-type methods (Fox et al., 2022; Etesami, 2026; Maheshwari et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Leonardos et al., 2021). These results demonstrate how suitable structure can fundamentally alter the computational and statistical difficulty of multi-agent learning. However, their structural assumptions may exclude systems in which agents have distinct objectives while their state processes evolve largely independently. This paper studies a different source of tractability: independence in the controlled state dynam-ics . We consider n-player stochastic games in which each player controls its own finite-state Markov chain. A player’s local transition kernel depends only on its own state and action, while strategic interactions occur through the reward functions, which may depend on the states and actions of all players. This model is natural in systems where individual agents have local physical dynamics but interact through a common environment, prices, congestion, or shared resources. Examples include wireless communication systems (Altman et al., 2007; Narayanan and Theagarajan, 2017), decentralized energy management (Etesami et al., 2018), and decentralized robotic navigation and coverage (Marden et al., 2009). Importantly, independence of the transition processes does not make the game strategically separable: a player’s payoff can still depend arbitrarily on the behavior of all other players. Our information model is deliberately restrictive. Each player observes only its own local state, chosen action, realized payoff, and subsequent local state; it neither observes the states or actions of the other players nor knows its own transition kernel. Thus, each player must simultaneously learn its local dynamics and adapt strategically to the other players from a single continuing trajectory. Moreover, we seek algorithms whose complexity is governed by the sizes and mixing properties of the local controlled chains rather than those of the product state–action space. This distinction is important because the joint state and action spaces grow exponentially with the number of players, even when each individual controlled chain is small. The independence structure permits a useful reformulation of the game in terms of stationary occupancy measures. Under stationary policies, independence of the local chains implies that the long-run joint state–action distribution factors into the product of local stationary occupancies (Ete-sami, 2024). Consequently, each player’s average reward is linear in its own occupancy measure when the occupancies of the other players are fixed. This converts the original dynamic interaction into a continuous-action static virtual game over local occupancy polytopes. When the transition kernels are unknown, however, these polytopes are themselves unknown and must be learned from data. This creates a coupled learning problem in which players must perform online optimization while simultaneously estimating the feasible regions over which the optimization is carried out. A further difficulty is that a genuinely online implementation cannot rely on the episodic sim-plifications commonly used to separate estimation from optimization. If policies are frozen for suf-ficiently long episodes, transition and payoff-gradient estimates can be formed from approximately stationary data before the next policy update. Along a continuing online trajectory, by contrast, policies change while data are being generated. The state distribution therefore tracks a moving stationary distribution, samples are temporally dependent, and confidence regions for the unknown kernels evolve asynchronously across players. Establishing regret guarantees in this setting requires controlling all of these effects without introducing synchronization among the players. 

> 2

The objective of this paper is to show that the local independence structure is strong enough to overcome these difficulties. We develop a fully online occupancy-based mirror-descent method in which each primitive interaction produces one transition/reward observation and one learning update. Each player maintains a confidence set for its unknown local transition kernel, which is up-dated only when a local state–action count reaches a dyadic threshold. Between such updates, the feasible occupancy region remains unchanged. This lazy confidence-update mechanism is useful both computationally and analytically: each player updates its model independently, its feasible re-gion changes only logarithmically many times, and no global episode boundaries or synchronization signals are required. For arbitrary reward functions, unconditional convergence to a NE cannot in general be ex-pected, given the computational hardness inherited from general-sum static games. We there-fore first analyze the algorithm through fixed-comparator regret and establish a high-probability 

eO(T −1/2) average regret bound against every fixed feasible stationary deviation. This directly yields an approximate coarse-correlated equilibrium guarantee for the empirical distribution of play (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006). Crucially, the resulting complexity depends only on local state and action dimensions, mixing parameters, and cover times, rather than on the cover time or cardi-nality of the exponentially larger joint state–action space. We then identify conditions under which the stronger conclusion of NE convergence can be recovered. Under an additional variational-stability condition (Mertikopoulos and Zhou, 2019) and diminishing step sizes, we show that the same decentralized fully online procedure converges in the last iterate to the unique stationary ϵ-NE with arbitrarily high probability. 

1.1 Related Work 

Our work lies at the intersection of learning in stochastic games, online learning in MDPs, and de-centralized primal-dual methods for stochastic games. For general stochastic games, much of the finite-sample literature has focused on settings with additional game-theoretic structure. Indepen-dent policy-gradient and Q-learning methods have been shown to converge in two-player zero-sum stochastic games under decentralized learning protocols (Daskalakis et al., 2020; Sayin et al., 2021). Another important line of work concerns MPGs, where policy-gradient and gradient-play methods exploit the potential structure to obtain global convergence guarantees for stationary NE policies (Zhang et al., 2022, 2024; Jordan et al., 2024; Leonardos et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023). These results leverage restrictions on payoff interactions to make equilibrium computa-tion tractable, whereas our setting allows general reward coupling and derives scalability from the independence of the players’ controlled transition processes. More recently, several works have combined independent transition dynamics with the Markov potential structure. (Nakhleh et al., 2025) studied fictitious play in product Markov games with Kullback–Leibler (KL) control costs and established asymptotic convergence to a NE for the subclass of product MPGs. (Jordan and Kamgarpour, 2026) studied MPGs with independent chains under a more restrictive information structure in which players only partially observe their local states, and established communication-free learning of approximate NE with quasi-polynomial sample and computational complexity. A related recent line of work studies decentralized learning in Markov α-potential games (Guo et al., 2025; Das and Eksin, 2024; Etesami, 2026), which generalize MPGs by allowing violations of the Markov potential property up to an additive error α. (Guo et al., 2025) derived Nash-regret guarantees for projected gradient-ascent and sequential maximum-improvement schemes under ex-

> 3

act Q-function oracle access. (Das and Eksin, 2024) introduced constrained α-Markov potential games, which further accommodate coupled constraints among players, and developed decentral-ized best-response-based algorithms with finite-time guarantees for approximate NE. A fully online decentralized learning framework with finite-time guarantees has only recently been developed for Markov α-potential games, where KL-projected natural policy-gradient algorithms achieve high-probability Nash-regret guarantees under both episodic and fully online learning (Etesami, 2026). Like the algorithm developed here, its fully online method operates along a continuing trajectory us-ing one realized sample per interaction. The two frameworks, however, exploit different sources of tractability: the former relies on an approximate Markov-potential structure in discounted Markov games, whereas our work imposes no potential structure on the rewards and instead exploits inde-pendent controlled chains in an average-reward setting. Our analysis also builds on ideas from online learning in MDPs. Occupancy measures pro-vide a convex representation of stationary policies and have been widely used to develop regret-minimization algorithms for adversarial MDPs (Cardoso et al., 2019). In particular, online mirror-descent methods over occupancy polytopes yield sublinear regret when losses vary across episodes, while confidence-set constructions extend such methods to unknown transition kernels (Rosenberg and Mansour, 2019). Related work has also considered bandit feedback with unknown dynamics in online MDPs (Jin et al., 2020). These single-agent techniques motivate our use of occupancy optimization and confidence regions, but do not directly apply to our setting: each player’s effective reward vector is generated endogenously by the evolving policies and state distributions of the other players, while all players learn simultaneously along their own continuing trajectories. More directly, stochastic games with independent state processes have been studied as a struc-tured class of decentralized dynamic games. (Altman et al., 2007) established equilibrium existence for cost-coupled stochastic games with independent state processes, and subsequent work character-ized stationary equilibria through mathematical programming formulations (Singh and Hemachan-dra, 2014). For learning problems, (Qiu et al., 2021) showed that, in two-player zero-sum stochastic games with independent chains, fictitious-play policy-optimization algorithms can achieve eO(√T )

regret, assuming that each player has knowledge of the opponent’s past strategies. (Etesami, 2024) introduced an occupancy-based dual formulation and developed decentralized dual-averaging pro-cedures for n-player stochastic games with independent chains. That work demonstrated that in-dependence can be leveraged as a source of computational tractability and established finite-time convergence guarantees in terms of the averaged Nikaido–Isoda gap (Nikaidˆ o and Isoda, 1955). Its main algorithms, however, assume known local transition kernels and operate in an episodic fashion, thereby requiring synchronization among players. Unknown transition dynamics were subsequently addressed by (Qin and Etesami, 2024), who combined the occupancy formulation with confidence sets and decentralized online mirror descent. Their algorithm learns the local transition models from observed trajectories and, under a variational-stability assumption, converges asymptotically to a stationary ϵ-NE. The learning protocol, however, remains episodic: policies are held fixed while sufficiently many local observations are collected, and an episode-ending signaling mechanism co-ordinates policy updates across players. Extending this framework to a fully online setting presents significant additional challenges, as continuous policy updates eliminate the stationary sampling environment provided by episodic learning and synchronization. The present paper removes this episodic architecture through delayed conditioning, coupling with frozen-policy dynamics, sensitivity bounds for policy and kernel movement, tracking of time-varying stationary occupancies, and careful treatment of asynchronous confidence-set updates. 1.2 Contributions 

We study stochastic games with independent controlled chains and unknown transition kernels and develop a fully decentralized online algorithm for learning stationary equilibrium policies. Our main contributions are as follows. • We develop a fully online, decentralized, and uncoordinated algorithm that uses one sample per time step. At each primitive time t, every player observes one local transition and realized payoff, updates its local transition counts, forms an importance-weighted payoff-gradient esti-mate, and takes one mirror-descent step. The algorithm requires no covering episodes, burn-in phases, or synchronization signals. Confidence sets are updated asynchronously only when a local state–action count reaches a dyadic threshold, so the feasible occupancy region remains fixed between updates and changes only logarithmically many times over a finite horizon. • For arbitrary reward functions, we establish a high-probability fixed-comparator regret bound of order eO(T −1/2) after a local-cover transient period. The bound depends only on local state and action dimensions, mixing parameters, and cover times, rather than on those of the exponentially larger joint state–action space. Consequently, the empirical distribution of play satisfies an approximate coarse-correlated-equilibrium guarantee. • We further identify a regime in which the stronger NE conclusion can be recovered. Under an additional variational-stability assumption and diminishing step sizes, we prove that the same fully online decentralized algorithm converges asymptotically in the last iterate to the unique stationary ϵ-NE with arbitrarily high probability. Thus, the finite-time no-regret guarantee requires no structural assumptions on the reward functions, while last-iterate NE convergence follows under stability of the induced occupancy game.

3 Preliminaries on Occupancy Measures, Dual Formulation, and Local Cover Times 

In this section, we provide an alternative dual formulation of the original stochastic game G based on occupancy measures (Altman, 2021). Intuitively, from player i’s perspective, its long-term ex-pected average payoff depends on the proportion of time it spends in each state si and takes each action ai, as captured by its occupancy measure. Thus, the policy optimization problem for player i

can be reformulated as an optimization problem over occupancy measures, where each player seeks to steer its chain toward high-reward states and actions. An advantage of optimizing over occupancy measures is that, due to the independence of the players’ internal chains, the payoff functions admit a simple closed-form that is easier to analyze than the original formulation in terms of policy vari-ables. Moreover, this simplified payoff structure allows us to leverage the rich literature on online learning and primal-dual methods to address the learning problem. We use this dual formulation to develop learning algorithms for finding a stationary ϵ-NE.

4 A Fully Online Decentralized Learning Algorithm 

In this section, we describe our fully online algorithm for stochastic games with unknown indepen-dent chains. Each primitive interaction produces exactly one transition/reward observation and one mirror-descent update. Unlike the episodic setting, which has been a standard approach in the pre-vious literature Etesami (2024); Qin and Etesami (2024), no player waits until all of its state–action pairs have been observed, there are no burn-in episodes, and no episode-end synchronization or co-ordination among the players is required. The transition kernels remain unknown and are estimated from the same single trajectory used for learning.

The regret is decomposed into a mirror-descent optimization error, an occupancy-tracking error, and a one-sample gradient-estimation error. We begin the analysis of Algorithm 1 by introducing the following terminology and filtration. 

5.1 True and Confidence-Feasible Occupancies 

We distinguish throughout between true-kernel feasible and confidence-feasible occupancy mea-sures. An occupancy measure qi is called true-kernel feasible if qi ∈ ∆i(Pi), i.e., if its induced transition kernel P qi equals the true transition kernel Pi. Its corresponding state–action and state marginal occupancies are denoted by ρi and νi, respectively. In contrast, an occupancy measure ˆqi

is called confidence-feasible if ˆqi ∈ ∆i,δ i (Pi), meaning that its induced transition kernel belongs to the confidence set Pi, i.e., P ˆqi ∈ P i. Its corresponding marginals are denoted by ˆρi and ˆνi.Accordingly, for each time t, let qti , ρ ti, ν ti denote the true-kernel feasible occupancies induced by the current stationary policy πti and the true kernel Pi. Thus, qti is the true-kernel feasible occupancy induced by (πti , P i), whereas ˆqti is the confidence-feasible occupancy that induces (πti , ˆP ti ), where, for simplicity, we write ˆP ti := P ˆqti ∈ P ti . Hence, throughout the analysis, unhatted occupancy quantities refer to the true kernel Pi, while hatted occupancy quantities refer to the confidence-feasible iterates maintained by the algorithm.

6 Asymptotic Last-Iterate Convergence to an ϵ-Nash Equilibrium 

It is known that, for general reward functions, computing a NE in stochastic games with independent chains is PPAD-hard even when the transition kernels are known (Etesami, 2024, Proposition 3.3). Therefore, under general reward functions, developing a scalable no-regret algorithm for obtaining a coarse correlated equilibrium, as established in the previous section, is perhaps the best one can hope for computationally. In this section, we show that if the reward functions possess additional structure, in the sense that the game admits a stable NE, then the same fully online algorithm converges with arbitrarily high probability to a stationary ϵ-NE of the original stochastic game. This complements the results of the previous section: not only does the algorithm provide a scalable no-regret guarantee for coarse correlated equilibria, but under the additional stability condition, the same algorithm converges to an ϵ-NE of the original stochastic game. To this end, we first introduce the following notion of equilibrium stability, which has been well studied and motivated for static games (Mertikopoulos and Zhou, 2019).

23 7 Real-World Applications 

The stochastic game model studied in this paper is relevant to multi-agent systems in which each player controls the evolution of a local stochastic state while interacting with other players through its payoff. This structure arises when the physical dynamics of individual agents are locally con-trolled, but their objectives are coupled through a shared resource, market, or environment. We illustrate this connection through three representative applications: energy management in smart grids, resource allocation in wireless networks, and decentralized robotic navigation and coverage. For these applications, the framework developed in this paper provides a fully decentralized way for agents to learn from a single continuing trajectory without knowing their local transition kernels or observing the states and actions of other players, thereby respecting users’ privacy and accom-modating communication or information bottlenecks that hinder coordination. Our results provide finite-time no-regret and asymptotic convergence guarantees whose complexity is governed by local state spaces and cover times, making them scalable and practical for large-scale systems. 

7.1 Energy Management in Smart Grids 

Consider a collection of n geographically distributed energy users, indexed by [n] = {1, . . . , n },interacting with a utility company. Each user acts as a prosumer : it can consume electricity from the grid while also producing energy locally through renewable sources such as solar panels or wind turbines. In addition, user i is equipped with a storage device of capacity C, allowing unused energy to be carried from one period to the next. Let sti denote the quantized energy stored by user i at the beginning of day t, and let hti denote the renewable energy harvested during that day. (c) Robotic navigation and coverage: robots with local controlled dynamics navigate through a shared environment while balancing individual navigation objectives with collective criteria such as congestion avoidance and spatial coverage. 

7.2 Power and Bandwidth Allocation in Wireless Networks 

A second example arises in decentralized wireless communication systems in which several trans-mitters compete for access to a common channel (Altman et al., 2007). Consider n users trans-mitting packets to one or more receivers over a shared wireless medium. For transmitter i, let sti

represent its local queue state at time t, describing, for example, the number of packets waiting to be transmitted. New packets arrive according to a local stochastic arrival process that is independent of the packet-generation processes of the other users. At each time t, transmitter i observes its own queue and selects an action ati, which may specify a transmission-power level, bandwidth allocation, transmission rate, or channel-access decision. Its queue then evolves according to the packets that arrive locally and the packets served as a consequence of its transmission decision. Thus, the action of each transmitter controls the evolution of its own queue state, while the underlying packet-arrival uncertainty is local to that user. The strategic interaction occurs through the shared communication medium. For example, the probability that player i successfully transmits a packet may depend on the transmission powers of all users, since simultaneous transmissions generate interference. More generally, the reward ri(st, a t) may account for successful throughput, transmission energy, queue-ing delay, packet loss, or congestion, and may therefore depend on the joint state–action profile. Channel conditions, such as background noise and available capacity, may further influence the realized payoff (see Figure 1–b). The resulting system separates local dynamics from strategic interaction in precisely the man-ner captured by the independent-chain model: each user’s queue evolves according to its own con-trolled local dynamics, whereas the performance resulting from a transmission decision depends on the simultaneous behavior of the other users. A transmitter can therefore seek to learn a stationary power- or bandwidth-allocation policy using only its local queue, selected transmission action, ob-served next queue state, and realized transmission payoff, without knowing its local transition law or observing the states and actions of the competing transmitters. 

25 7.3 Decentralized Robotic Navigation and Coverage 

A third application arises in decentralized robotic navigation and coverage, where a collection of autonomous agents moves through a common environment while collectively monitoring, explor-ing, or covering a region of interest. Such cooperative-control problems have been studied using potential, state-based potential, and distributed welfare games (Marden et al., 2009; Marden, 2012; Marden and Wierman, 2013). To connect this setting to the independent-chain model, suppose that robot i has a quantized local state sti describing its position, heading, velocity, battery level, sens-ing status, or other locally available information relevant to its motion and operation. At time t,the robot observes its current local state and selects a local control action ati, such as a movement direction, velocity adjustment, or sensing decision. Its next state is then generated according to an unknown controlled transition kernel Pi(st+1  

> i

| sti, a ti), which may capture uncertainty in ac-tuation, localization, terrain conditions, sensor measurements, or other robot-specific disturbances encountered during operation (see Figure 1–c). When these disturbances are independent across robots, each robot’s state evolves according to its own locally controlled stochastic dynamics, and the resulting system has the independent controlled-chain structure considered in this paper. The robots nevertheless interact strategically through their rewards, which may account for travel time, energy consumption, congestion, collision avoidance, or progress toward a destination. The reward may also incorporate a collective coverage objective. For example, the workspace can be partitioned into Voronoi cells, with each robot responsible for monitoring its associated region, and the collective reward can favor configurations that cover a larger fraction of the workspace, reduce the aggregate distance between points and their nearest robots, or place robots near the cen-troids of their Voronoi regions (Marden et al., 2009; Marden and Wierman, 2013). Accordingly, the reward ri(st, a t) can combine individual navigation and energy costs with team-dependent criteria such as coverage, congestion, and collision avoidance. Thus, although each robot controls only its local dynamics, its reward can depend on the configuration and actions of the entire robot popula-tion. The resulting equilibrium policies can therefore represent decentralized motion strategies that balance local navigation costs with collective objectives such as efficient spatial coverage. 

8 Conclusion 

We developed a fully online one-sample occupancy-measure learning algorithm for stochastic games with unknown independent controlled chains. The algorithm operates along a single continuing tra-jectory, using one local transition and realized payoff per time step, while players update their policies and learn their unknown transition kernels asynchronously using only local information. Under uniform ergodicity, each individual controlled chain has a finite worst-case expected cover time Hi, which enters the finite-time complexity as a local problem-dependent parameter. A frozen-policy block comparison, together with the slow movement of mirror descent, transfers fixed-policy coverage and mixing properties to the time-inhomogeneous online trajectory. The resulting high-probability fixed-comparator regret decreases at the rate eO(T −1/2) after a local-cover transient pe-riod. For arbitrary reward functions, this regret guarantee further implies that the empirical distribu-tion of play approaches a coarse-correlated equilibrium. Because transition estimation and delayed moving-chain comparisons are performed separately on each local state space Si, neither the cover time nor the cardinality of the product state space Q 

> i

Si enters the complexity. Under an additional variational-stability condition and diminishing stepsizes, the same fully online scheme further yields asymptotic last-iterate convergence to a stationary ϵ-NE.
